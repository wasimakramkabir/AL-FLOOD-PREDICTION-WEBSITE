Stage 01

Problem identification

First, understand the real-world problem your project is trying to help with. A clear problem statement guides every later design decision.

🌧️

Heavy rainfall

Intense or prolonged rain can increase runoff and raise river or surface-water levels.

🌊

River overflow

When water levels exceed the capacity of river channels, nearby areas may be inundated.

🏙️

Urban drainage

Blocked or insufficient drainage can contribute to waterlogging in towns and cities.

📢

Warning challenges

People need timely, understandable, location-relevant information to prepare for possible flooding.

Problem statement

Our project proposes: a web application that analyses environmental data—such as rainfall and river water level—to estimate flood risk and display understandable risk information and preparedness guidance.

Project objectives

  1. Identify useful environmental factors related to flood risk.
  2. Collect and prepare suitable historical or sample data.
  3. Build and evaluate a beginner-level machine-learning model.
  4. Display the model's estimated risk category on a web dashboard.
  5. Present general preparedness information for users.

Scope and limitations

In scope

Educational prototype, dataset analysis, model evaluation, dashboard and clearly labelled risk estimates.

Out of scope

Replacing official forecasts, emergency services, or government alerts; guaranteeing that a flood will or will not occur.

Our proposed contribution

Prototype idea: combine selected, available environmental inputs in one educational dashboard and show a model-generated risk category with its limitations. The value is in demonstrating a complete data-to-dashboard workflow, not claiming better accuracy than operational systems.

Research questions for the team

Stage 03

AI concepts and techniques

Now learn how a machine-learning model can use examples from past data to estimate a flood-risk category for new input data.

What is Artificial Intelligence?

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a broad area of computing focused on building systems that perform tasks that usually require human-like reasoning, pattern recognition or decision-making.

What is Machine Learning?

Machine Learning (ML) is a part of AI. Instead of writing every decision as a fixed rule, we train an algorithm using examples so it can learn patterns in data.

Simple example: give a model many past records containing rainfall, river level and the recorded flood outcome. The model learns relationships in those examples and estimates a risk category for a new record.

Key terms

Dataset

A collection of records used to study and train the model.

Features

Input columns, such as rainfall amount and river water level.

Label

The known outcome for a record, such as a documented flood-risk class.

Training

The process in which an algorithm learns patterns from training examples.

Testing

Checking performance on separate data the model did not train on.

Prediction

The model's estimated class for a new input record.

How will we evaluate the model?

For a classification model, we can report accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and a confusion matrix. For flood-risk work, recall for the high-risk class is especially important to examine, while also checking false alarms and missed events.

Important: A model's output is an estimate, not a guaranteed forecast. Its reliability depends on representative, correctly labelled data, suitable validation and ongoing monitoring.
